Clarity · Life Center · Round 1, UX directions

A long list, held lightly

Life Center today is one list sorted by when. Every task you type, and every task Clarity finds in a note, adds a row, and every row asks to be read and decided on. Here are five ways to hold the same list so it stops pressing on the person looking at it, a new task card, and a bank of smaller ideas that work with any of them. Each direction has a working phone you can tap.

Where we are

Why a long list weighs so much

Life Center is calm row by row. The trouble is length. Here it is with the thirty-odd tasks a few busy weeks leave behind.

Life Center today: area chips, the slipped card, then Today, This week, Later and Someday, each a card of rows.

Every row is a decision nobody has made yet.Seeing forty tasks means forty small questions of "now, or not?". Most of the effort is spent before anything is done.
Everything is the same size.A two-minute call and a weekend's job take one identical row, so the list can't say how much a day really holds.
The list grows faster than intention.Find tasks turns a note's passing thoughts into rows. A list can fill up without anyone choosing to fill it.
Reasons fade."Ask Sam about the brackets" made sense on Monday. Three weeks later it's a puzzle: which Sam, which brackets, why?
Time words ask for arithmetic."Later" and "Someday" mean holding a calendar in mind, which is the very part that is hard.
Nothing leaves gracefully.Old tasks keep their full volume. The only way out is Delete, which feels like admitting failure.

For people living with mild cognitive impairment, each of these leans on working memory and on planning, starting and switching between things, which are the abilities under the most strain. A long list doesn't only look busy. It uses up the capacity the person needs to act on it.

For any direction

Six rules

Each direction below is a different shape. These are the rules every one of them keeps.

Hold more than you show.The app can know about everything. The screen only needs what helps right now.
Ask one thing at a time.Decisions come singly, as a tap, never as a page of choices.
Give every task a size, a place and a reason.A quick call should never look like a weekend's job, and no task should become a puzzle.
Let distance soften detail.Next month doesn't need the same ink as this afternoon.
Make letting go safe.Nothing is lost. What you let go of rests, and can come back with one tap.
Today is for doing. Life is for deciding.Today stays the place to act. Life Center becomes the place where the list is looked after.

These build on what revamp 5 has already settled: content on cards, one accent, no counts, pictures only for moments, buttons that answer in place. They reuse Catch up's peeking card, round 3's thread and parts of the day, and the "feel alive" research's list of things to leave out: streaks, points and guilt.

Asks: what's on my plate?

Stacks

Tasks gather into piles by what they belong to. You see the top of each pile, never the whole pile.

Try it

Or tap a pile's name to fan it out, and a ring to finish its top card.

People overwhelmed by paper don't read every sheet. They make piles. A pile hides everything but its top sheet, and its thickness tells you roughly how much is there without counting. Stacks gives Life Center the same move. Clarity groups tasks into threads (a project, a person, a place, or the note they came from) and shows each thread as one card, with the edges of the rest peeking out underneath.

How it works

  • The top card is the next step. Only it is written out in full. Its pile's name says why it matters: "Kitchen shelves", "Mum", "Out and about".
  • Thickness, not numbers. One, two or three edges say a little, some, or a lot.
  • Tap to fan out. The pile opens in place to show everything in it, in order. Tap again and it gathers back.
  • Tick the top, the next one rises. The pile gets thinner. When it's empty it says so, and leaves.
  • Tuck under sends the top card to the bottom of its own pile, for "not this one yet".
  • Set aside moves a whole pile into a drawer at the foot of the page: still there, out of sight. Someday starts in the drawer.
  • Clarity suggests piles from the notes and words tasks share. Anything can be moved by dropping it on another pile.
  • A dated card can't hide. On its day it rises to the top of its pile, so time still counts.

With a long list

15 tasksFive or six piles, mostly single cards. It reads like today's list.
60 tasksAbout ten piles, a screen and a bit. The page is as long as it was at thirty.
200 tasksA dozen piles in view and the rest set aside. Growth makes piles thicker, not the page longer.

Why it suits Clarity

Reading drops to one line per thread. The thread's name carries the context a lone task loses: "Ask Sam about the brackets" sits under "Kitchen shelves". It extends Catch up's peeking card, which people already know, and it's the direction that compresses a long list the most.

Risks

  • Clarity's grouping will sometimes be wrong, so moving a card must be easy and forgiving.
  • Some tasks belong to two threads (Mum's birthday lunch is People and Plans).
  • What's underneath can feel hidden. The dated-card rule and fanning out have to earn trust.

What we'd test

  • Can people find a named task faster or slower than in today's list?
  • Do they trust what's underneath, or open every pile to check?
  • Do Clarity's piles match how people describe their own lives?
Asks: when does it matter?

Near & Far

Time is shown as distance. Today stands close and clear; next month waits, small and soft, on the horizon.

Try it

With a mouse, drag down inside the phone to walk toward the horizon. Tap a day's sky to see how full it is.

"Later" and "Someday" ask the reader to do calendar arithmetic. Distance doesn't. Everyone knows that what's close needs attention and what's far away can wait. Near & Far draws Life Center as a view into depth: today's tasks in the foreground at full size, this week smaller behind them, later as plain words, someday as a soft line near the horizon. You travel forward by pulling the far things toward you.

How it works

  • Depth carries the date. Nothing needs a heading to say it's far away; it looks far away.
  • Detail fades with distance. Near things are cards with times and checks. Far things are just their words, quieter, with no details.
  • Pull to travel. Walk toward the horizon and the far tasks grow into cards as they arrive, while today passes behind you.
  • Move in time by moving in space. Flick a task up to send it further away; draw a far one down to bring it near.
  • Weather for the week. A strip of skies shows how full each day is: clear, a few clouds, busy. Tap a cloudy day and Clarity offers to move something to a clear one.
  • At your feet. What slipped doesn't turn red. It waits at your feet, to pick up or let go.

With a long list

15 tasksToday's cards, and a few words on the horizon.
60 tasksThe near view doesn't change. The far bands are longer lines of words.
200 tasksThe horizon thickens into a haze, "more in the haze". You only read it by travelling there.

Why it suits Clarity

It turns the sections Life Center already has into something felt rather than read, and it follows round 3's "sky" look and the time-of-day pictures. The far future can't overwhelm anyone, because it is literally small.

Risks

  • Small, soft words are harder to read. Far words must stay legible with Larger text, and a tap brings any of them near.
  • Moving depth can upset people sensitive to motion. Under Reduce Motion the bands cross-fade.
  • Pulling to travel isn't obvious the first time.

What we'd test

  • Do people know where "next Thursday" is without looking for a date?
  • Does the haze feel calming, or like things are hidden?
  • Do the skies read as how busy a day is, or as the actual weather?
Asks: where can I do it?

Rooms

Every task lives somewhere in a drawn house. You remember the kitchen, so you find what's in the kitchen.

Try it

Tap any room. The little notes are tasks; a sun means something there is for today.

A place is easier to recognise than a word is to recall, and tying things to rooms (the method of loci) is one of the oldest memory techniques there is. Rooms turns areas into places. The desk holds work and paperwork, the hall table holds things to take out, the mantelpiece holds people, the bedside holds health, the attic holds someday. Life Center opens on the house. You don't read a list; you look into a room.

How it works

  • A house, not a list. Each room shows its tasks as little notes. A busy room looks cluttered and a calm one looks tidy, with no numbers.
  • Look into a room. Tap it and the house zooms in; the room's tasks come up as notes to tick.
  • Where are you? "Going out" lights the hall and the garden and says what to take. "At my desk" lights the study.
  • Clarity places new tasks by their words: a call goes to the mantelpiece, buying goes by the front door. Drag a note to another room to move it.
  • Your house, your rooms. Rename them, add the allotment or the car, take away the attic.
  • Today glows. A small sun on a room means something in it is for today.

With a long list

15 tasksA tidy house, a note or two in most rooms.
60 tasksBusier rooms, but each room on its own is a short list.
200 tasksCluttered rooms invite a tidy, one room at a time. The house never gets longer.

Why it suits Clarity

It speaks Clarity's picture language (flat colour inside a dark outline). It swaps abstract area names for places people already know, which matters when the right word won't come: "the thing in the kitchen" is enough. And context, which today's list ignores, becomes the way in: what can I do from here?

Risks

  • Done badly it looks childish. It needs the same restraint as the existing pictures.
  • Homes differ: a flat, shared housing, a care home. Rooms must be editable, and work for people who don't think in space.
  • It's the costliest to build and the hardest to make work with VoiceOver ("Kitchen, a few things, one for today").

What we'd test

  • A week later, can people say which room a task is in?
  • Does it feel adult and warm, or like a game?
  • Do people use "Going out", and does it help?
Asks: how much can I carry?

Stones

Tasks have weight, and a day is a bowl. You plan by what a day can hold, not by how many things are on it.

Try it

Or tap a stone on the shore, then a day's bowl. Tap a bowl on its own to see what's in it.

A list says nothing about size. A two-minute call and a weekend's job take the same row, so a day can be planned three times over and still look fine at nine in the morning. Stones gives every task a weight: a pebble, a stone or a rock. Days are bowls. You put stones into a day until it looks full, and when it's full a new stone won't settle. It rolls back, and Clarity points to a day with room.

How it works

  • Three weights. A pebble is a few minutes, a stone an hour or so, a rock a big job. Clarity guesses from the words; a tap changes it.
  • A bowl for each day. Bowls fill by weight. The eye reads "nearly full" with no number.
  • A full day says so. A stone that won't fit rolls back, and a day with room glows.
  • Break a rock. Tap a big job and Clarity offers to break it into stones, each a real first step.
  • The shore. Unplanned tasks wait on the shore. Things untouched for a while lie further down the beach, paler. They aren't lost and they don't shout.
  • The cairn. Finished stones go onto a cairn that grows through the week: a quiet record of what was carried, never counted.

With a long list

15 tasksA few stones on the shore, bowls half full.
60 tasksA wider shore. The nearest stones are labelled; the far ones are texture.
200 tasksMost of the beach is pale and far. Planning still only touches a handful of stones.

Why it suits Clarity

Capacity is the idea today's list is missing, and overplanning is a common route to an evening that feels like failure. Weight also answers "what can I manage today?" at a glance, which is the question someone with low energy is really asking.

Risks

  • Guessing size is hard, for Clarity and for people.
  • Dragging is hard with a tremor. Every move needs a tap-then-tap way too, as here.
  • A physical metaphor can tip into whimsy. It has to stay quiet.

What we'd test

  • Do people plan fewer, more realistic days?
  • Do they feel better at the end of the day than with a list?
  • Can they read a bowl's fullness without help?
Asks: what should I do now?

Held

Clarity holds the whole list. Life Center asks for one small decision at a time, and the rest stays safely out of sight.

Try it

Most of the work of a long list is deciding: what matters, what's next, what can go. That's exactly the work that's hardest under cognitive strain. Held moves the deciding into short, kind conversations. Life Center opens on the one thing that makes most sense right now, with its reason and a few gentle ways out, and three short rituals look after the rest. Every answer is a tap. Nothing needs typing, or the right word.

How it works

  • One thing, with a reason. Chosen from the time, today's plans and how you said you feel: "It's the only thing with a time this afternoon."
  • Gentle doors. Something else, Not today, or Low on energy, which offers something smaller.
  • Choose tomorrow with me (about a minute, in the evening). Cards are dealt one at a time, yes or not tomorrow, until the day looks full.
  • Put today away (half a minute). What didn't happen goes to tomorrow, next week, or a rest, with no judgement.
  • A two-minute tidy (once a week). A few of the oldest things, one at a time: does this still matter?
  • Show everything is always one tap away, so nothing feels hidden.
  • It says what it holds in words, not numbers: "I'm keeping everything else safe."

With a long list

15 tasksThe one thing and a ritual or two. Like a friend with a notebook.
60 tasksExactly the same. The list's length never reaches the screen.
200 tasksStill the same. The weekly tidy brings the oldest things up for a decision before they pile up.

Why it suits Clarity

It takes the planning load off the person and puts it on the app, which is what an assistive tool is for. The rituals give the week a rhythm, and routine is one of the strongest supports for memory. It's also a natural home for Clarity's AI, which already writes summaries and first steps.

Risks

  • It can feel like being managed. How much it leads should be a setting.
  • When the pick is wrong, trust drops. "Something else" must be instant and never sulk.
  • Hidden things can feel lost. Every move says where the task went.
  • Rituals can become chores. They're short and optional, and skipping one costs nothing.

What we'd test

  • Do people feel looked after, or managed?
  • Do the right tasks still get done?
  • After two weeks, have the rituals become habits?

Recommendation

My pick for round 2

Held as the front door, with Stacks behind "Show everything".

Held deals with the root of the overwhelm: the person never faces the whole list unless they ask to. It turns the hardest work (deciding, planning, letting go) into taps, and it gives Clarity's AI a clear, useful job. But a held list must be easy to inspect, or things seem to vanish. Stacks is the best way to show everything: it shortens a long list the most, and each pile's name keeps its tasks' reasons.

Then borrow the best single idea from each of the others:

  • From Stones, the bowl. In "Choose tomorrow with me" the day fills by weight, and says when it's full.
  • From Near & Far, the haze. The set-aside drawer and Someday read as far away: smaller, softer, no details.
  • From Rooms, "Going out?". A door in Held that says what to take with you.
  • For every card: turn it over, not everything is a task, resting not deleting, and a fresh start.

Rooms and Stones are the boldest ideas here and the least certain. They deserve a paper test with three to five people before anyone builds them in full.

To steer round 2

Questions for you

How much should Clarity lead?Held decides what comes next. Some people will love being looked after; others will feel managed. Should leading be a setting, or part of the design?
Can Life Center hide things by default?Every direction shows less than it holds. Is that acceptable as long as everything is one tap away?
Should areas survive?Stacks and Rooms both replace areas with something more concrete (threads, places). Keep areas as a filter underneath, or let them go?
Is weight worth asking for?Stones, and the size on the new card, need a weight for every task. Clarity can guess it. Is a wrong guess acceptable?
Are care partners in scope?Hand it on needs sharing, which Clarity doesn't have yet. It matters a lot to this audience. Now, or later?
